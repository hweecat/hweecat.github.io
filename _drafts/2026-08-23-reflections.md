It's been a long while (3+ years) since I've written a long-form reflection post.

Not because I have not been reflecting on my life, but because the thoughts weigh too heavily on me to pen down and admit that I even have these thoughts in a platform that is supposed to showcase myself.

The version of the reflections I shared on LinkedIn? Self-censored, filtered and polished for the professional audience - the recruiters, hiring managers, and people I've connected with from events, educational years and community work.

I've shared snippets about my disillusionment with my career trajectory, the grief I've been holding on from my past experiences, and the lessons I've learned along the way. Having a space that is separate from the expectations of a professional platform allows me to be more honest with my thoughts without the pressure of maintaining a perfect image that is suitable for professional consumption.

Where do I begin?

## How Much Do Titles Matter Across Companies? A Personal Introspective

In September 2021, I received my first Senior Data Engineer offer from a Southeast Asian regional tech company when I was 8 months into working at a fintech scaleup as a mid-level Data Engineer. It was a step up in terms of title, scope and prestige - with a catch: it was before its IPO via a SPAC merger. While I did not accept the initial offer, the allure of the gravitas that comes with the "Senior" title in a higher-tier company was too strong to ignore - especially when I already felt that I was lagging far behind my peers in terms of career success.

When I finally joined that regional tech company as a founding Senior Data Engineer for the Indonesia Digibank project in August 2022, it felt like I was finally gaining more respect from my peers in the tech industry due to the perceived seniority and prestige of my position as a young technical leader for a fast-growing business vertical that aims to deliver financial inclusion in the largest country within ASEAN (how naive I was!). Having the "Senior" title made a significant difference in how my credibility as an engineer and technical leader was perceived by technical and non-technical folks who are not familiar with my career journey prior to that role - to the point where I became increasingly uncomfortable with **how the perception of my credibility is likely to be fragile and conditional upon my title and continued association with a well-known regional tech company**.

After 3+ years of leading data platform, data product and machine learning platform initiatives, I still had nothing visible to show for in terms of getting a promotion to Lead and proving to myself that I'm on an upward career trajectory. In fact, I "lost" the seniority that I fought so hard to hold on to for the past 5 years - only to make a lateral move and "start over" again as a mid-level individual contributor in Big Tech.

Being aware of the seniority roller-coaster in tech and witnessing it happening to my peers in the tech industry is one thing; what I did not expect was how I reacted when I experienced the roller-coaster myself.

The title "loss" initially felt like a personal failure (especially since it was a downlevel offer with a relatively modest increase in compensation) even though it was a fair trade-off that I'm already mentally prepared to accept to move "up the food chain" when I applied for the role. The mind was 70% willing to accept the "lower" title as it knew that the lateral move is the reset I need to extend my career runway in tech, but the ego was reluctant to let go of the fear of losing the "hard-won" respect and credibility that I had fought really hard to establish since starting over from ground zero 10 years ago. I needed a stronger mindset reframe to convince my ego to let go of that fear and take another massive leap of faith to gain more technical expertise and start learning as an engineer again.

Sharing honestly about this fear with a few trusted peers and more experienced technical leaders who have navigated similar transitions opened up my perspectives on career ladders across companies and how career trajectories are perceived in the tech industry. Between career growth, total compensation and title, what do I choose for a better outcome for myself in the longer term if I could only choose 2 out of 3?

It is undeniable that titles carry weight in terms of visibility, credibility and external perception - my perspectives are perceived as more credible as a technical leader with a "senior" title in a well-known regional tech company. Does that "senior" title in regional tech translate across companies operating at a global scale with more established practices? Not necessarily. The scope and impact of the work, as well as the complexity of navigating organizational dynamics, are different across companies operating at different maturity phases, cultural norms, and scale. If this increased scope comes with higher total compensation, why not bite the bullet, get my foot in the door, and focus on growing myself as a technical leader while reframing my non-linear title progression as a "level up" in terms of scope and opening up more options for my future self?

## "Starting Over" is not Starting from Scratch

10 years ago, I started over by walking away from a toxic relationship that almost destroyed my life and a research career that has become misaligned with my shifting interests towards computational sciences, and picking up coding again to prepare for my graduate studies in computational sciences. I shut out my emotions and cut off almost everyone except my closest friends from my life at that time, because I felt that I needed to focus all my remaining energy towards starting from scratch without bearing the burden of previous histories and forcing myself to come up with socially-acceptable responses to questions from my peers from high school and university on how I'm doing in my life. The long, painful journey of rebuilding my life, confidence, and career from "rock bottom" has gradually buried the shame I still instinctively feel in falling behind my peers who are not only ahead of me in terms of career trajectory but also in their life milestones.

The interesting thing about life is that your past will eventually catch up to your present in a different form. I rejoined the aviation industry as a data engineer after my graduate studies by happenstance, only to leave the aviation industry after 2 years due to the global pandemic. A few of my peers eventually reached out to me after seeing my updates on speaking at tech conferences and navigating a turbulent-yet-upward-looking career trajectory in tech as a young technical leader in fintech, wondering how they could emulate my path while I constantly questioned myself on whether I'm even remotely successful at rebuilding my life fast enough to catch up with their level of success.

The key lesson I've learnt from the process of rebuilding is that "starting over" does not mean I have to start from scratch. A part of me never stopped feeling like I'm at least 4 years behind my peers in terms of my career and life trajectory because my past experiences in aerospace engineering kept getting discounted by hiring maangers and recruiters in the tech industry, even after I've done my best to connect the dots and reframe my interdisciplinary background in aerospace engineering and business as my unique value-add. The reality is more nuanced than what it appears on the timeline, because I'm starting from experience - the hard-fought wins, gut-wrenching lessons, and everything in between.

The core principles that guide my problem-solving and decision-making approach as a data engineer and technical leader are rooted in my prior experiences as a research engineer and technologist for aircraft engine systems where I worked with engineering programme managers who continuously invest in building up their domain knowledge to build empathy with their engineers and researchers. My system design and data architecture instincts are built upon systems thinking and computational engineering fundamentals that I developed from my interdisplinary engineering education. My ability to dive really deep with intense rigour, zoom out to see the broader picture, and adapt quickly across multiple domains are shaped by my industrial research background and 0-1 experiences.

For people who are not familiar with how career levels work in tech, it might look like I'm falling further behind by starting over again as a mid-level individual contributor after all the efforts invested into rebuilding and getting back into an upward trajectory for the past 10 years. It's not - I walked away from a role and company that is no longer aligned with my core values and principles as a technical leader after years of tension and grief, and reset my career trajectory on a much stronger baseline position than where I initially started. Letting go feels like failure, and I would be lying to myself if I were to claim to have fully gotten over the grief and trauma of being left behind to pick up the pieces alone while putting up a confident facade to the outside world. The reframe that I'm currently adopting is that walking away from what no longer fits is an act of self-respect and courage to make room for something better.

In this new season of "starting over", I'm learning to harness the lessons from my past experiences, deepen my technical expertise in the agentic AI era, and move past survival mode so that I can feel more alive again and thrive as a much healthier technical leader. This time, I'm not shutting out the difficult emotions or cutting people off from my life, because my gut instinct tells me that I'm not starting from scratch and alone in this rebuilding journey this time - even when it feels like nobody is rooting for me or understands what I'm doing. 

## Vulnerability as a strength - or a weapon against you?

The idea that vulnerability is a sign of weakness is deeply ingrained in many professional cultures. I've carried this belief for years, convinced that showing any form of uncertainty, emotional response, neurodivergence, or human frailty would undermine my authority and competence in professional settings. I've lived with this fear for so long that I didn't even realize how much it was holding me back until the cognitive and emotional load of maintaining clear separations between the multiple personas that I maintain in my personal and professional spheres became increasingly exhausting to the point of spillover into intense internal conflict in how I show up as a person and a leader in each of the spheres.

It's true that vulnerability forms the very foundation of authentic leadership and meaningful connections. However, the paradox is that vulnerability can be both a strength and a weapon, depending on how it's framed and who receives it. In a culture that rewards certainty and control, showing vulnerability can feel like exposing a wound that could be exploited by peers and managers to undermine your credibility. Yet, when shared thoughtfully in a culture that embraces uncertainty and rewards informed risk-taking, vulnerability humanizes us and invites collaboration and trust rather than competition and self-preservation. The challenge is in identifying workplaces and teams where vulnerability is rewarded rather than punished.

One of the most powerful lessons I've learned is that vulnerability requires context and intentionality. It's not about broadcasting every insecurity or personal crisis without boundaries or discretion.

It's about sharing the battle scars that shaped my decisions and growth, even when it feels like exposing my human fallability and risking my credibility as a leader.

It's about having the courage to admit my mistakes in front of senior leaders and committing to concrete actions to rebuild trust with my team and stakeholders, even when it feels like I could get fired for incompetence by accepting responsibility for the outcome.

It's about having the humility to ask for help from trusted peers and leaders who have more expertise in the topics I need input on and learn from their experiences, even when it feels risky and embarrassing to do so.

I'm in the process of learning that vulnerability is not a liability to be hidden, but a bridge that connects my personal experiences to the ongoing challenges we all face in an increasingly volatile and uncertain world. By embracing the discomfort of vulnerability instead of fighting against it, I find that I'm not only showing up more authentically but also becoming more resilient in navigating my professional and personal turbulences with greater self-awareness and empathy. There are days where I still feel compelled to show one of the many personas I've developed over the years to navigate how to show up in various professional and social situations.

## Moving Forward

As I continue to navigate this agentic AI era and the evolving landscape of technical leadership, these reflections serve as a compass for how I choose to engage with my work and my community. The journey is far from linear, and the title of "Senior" or "Lead" is now just one of many markers along a path defined by continuous learning, emotional resilience, and the courage to be human in a field that often values perfection over presence.

I share these thoughts not as a confession of failure, but as an invitation for others who might be walking similar paths. If you're feeling the weight of titles, the pressure to start over, or the fear of vulnerability, know that these are not signs of weakness or incompetence—they're indicators of a career that's still growing and evolving. And in that growth, there's room for both technical excellence and human authenticity.
