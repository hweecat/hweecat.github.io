It's been a long while (3+ years) since I've written a long-form reflection post.

Not because I have not been reflecting on my life, but because the thoughts weigh too heavily on me to pen down and admit that I even have these thoughts in a platform that is supposed to showcase myself.

The version of the reflections I shared on LinkedIn? Self-censored, filtered and polished for the professional audience - the recruiters, hiring managers, and people I've connected with from events, educational years and community work.

I've shared snippets about my disillusionment with my career trajectory, the grief I've been holding on from my past experiences, and the lessons I've learned along the way. Having a space that is separate from the expectations of a professional platform allows me to be more honest with my thoughts without the pressure of maintaining a perfect image that is suitable for professional consumption.

Where do I begin?

## How Much Do Titles Matter Across Companies? A Personal Introspective

In September 2021, I received my first Senior Data Engineer offer from Grab when I was 8 months into working at a fintech scaleup as a mid-level Data Engineer. It was a step up in terms of title, scope and prestige - with a catch: it was before Grab's IPO via a SPAC merger. While I did not accept the initial offer, the allure of the gravitas that comes with the "Senior" title in a higher-tier company was too strong to ignore - especially when I already felt that I was lagging far behind my peers in terms of career success.

When I finally joined Grab as a founding Senior Data Engineer for the Indonesia Digibank project in August 2022, it felt like I was finally gaining more respect from my peers in the tech industry due to the perceived seniority and prestige of my position as a young technical leader for a fast-growing business vertical that aims to deliver financial inclusion in the largest country within ASEAN (how naive I was!). Having the Senior title made a significant difference in how my credibility as an engineer and technical leader was perceived by technical and non-technical folks who are not familiar with my career journey prior to Grab - to the point where I became increasingly uncomfortable with **how the perception of my credibility is likely to be fragile and conditional upon my title and continued association with a well-known regional tech company**.

After 3+ years of leading data platform, data product and machine learning platform initiatives, I still had nothing visible to show for in terms of getting a promotion to Lead and proving to myself that I'm on an upward career trajectory. In fact, I "lost" the seniority that I fought so hard to hold on to for the past 5 years - only to "start over" again as a mid-level individual contributor in a global tech company.

Being aware of the seniority roller-coaster in tech and witnessing it happening to my peers in the tech industry is one thing; what I did not expect was how I reacted when I experienced the roller-coaster myself.

The title "loss" initially felt like a personal failure (especially since it was a down-level offer with a relatively modest increase in compensation) even though it was a fair trade-off that I'm already mentally prepared to accept to move "up the food chain". The mind was 70% willing to accept the "lower" title, but the ego was reluctant to let go of the fear of losing the "hard-won" respect and credibility that I had worked really hard to establish since starting over from ground zero 10 years ago. I needed a stronger mindset reframe to convince my ego to let go of that fear and take another massive leap of faith to gain more technical expertise and start learning as an engineer again.

Sharing honestly about this fear with a few trusted peers and more experienced technical leaders who have navigated similar transitions opened up my perspectives on career ladders across companies and how career trajectories are perceived in the tech industry. Between career growth, total compensation and title, what do I choose for a better outcome for myself in the longer term if I could only choose 2 out of 3? It is undeniable that titles carry weight in terms of visibility and external perception - my perspectives are perceived as more credible as a technical leader with a "senior" title in a well-known regional tech company. Does that "senior" title in regional tech translate across companies operating at a global scale with more established practices? Not necessarily.

It made me question whether titles and external validation were the right metrics for measuring progress, or if there were other ways to define professional fulfillment.

## "Starting Over" is not Starting from Scratch

10 years ago, I started over by walking away from a toxic relationship that almost destroyed my life and a research career that has become misaligned with my shifting interests towards computational sciences and picking up coding again to prepare for my graduate studies in computational sciences. I shut out my emotions and cut off almost everyone except my closest friends from my life at that time, because I felt that I needed to start from scratch without bearing the burden of previous histories and forcing myself to come up with socially-acceptable responses to questions from my peers from high school and university on how I'm doing in my life. The long, painful journey of rebuilding my life, confidence, and career from "rock bottom" has gradually buried the shame I still instinctively feel in falling behind my peers who are not only ahead of me in terms of career trajectory but also in their life milestones.

The interesting thing about life is that your past will eventually catch up to your present in a different form. I rejoined the aviation industry as a data engineer after my graduate studies by happenstance. A few of my peers eventually reached out to me after seeing my updates on speaking at tech conferences and navigating a turbulent-yet-upward-looking career trajectory in tech as a young technical leader in fintech.

The key lesson I've learnt from the process of rebuilding is that "starting over" does not mean I have to start from scratch. A part of me never stopped feeling like I'm at least 4 years behind my peers in terms of my career and life trajectory because my past experiences in aerospace engineering kept getting discounted in the tech industry, even after I've done my best to connect the dots and reframe my aerospace engineering background as my unique value-add. The reality is more nuanced than what it appears on the timeline, because I'm starting from experience - the hard-fought wins, gut-wrenching lessons, and everything in between.

The core principles that guide my problem-solving and decision-making approach as a data engineer and technical leader are rooted in my prior experiences as a research engineer and technologist for aircraft engine systems where I worked with engineering programme managers who continuously invest in building up their domain knowledge to build empathy with their engineers and researchers. My system design and data architecture instincts are built upon system design and computational engineering fundamentals that I developed from my interdisplinary engineering education.

It might look like I'm falling further behind by starting over again as a mid-level individual contributor after all the efforts invested into rebuilding for the past 10 years. It's not - I walked away from a role and company that is no longer aligned with my core values and principles as a technical leader after years of tension and grief, and reset my career trajectory on a much stronger baseline position than where I initially started. In this new season of "starting over", I'm learning to harness the lessons from my past experiences, deepen my technical expertise in the agentic AI era, and move past survival mode so that I can feel more alive again and thrive as a much healthier technical leader. This time, I'm not shutting out the negative emotions or cutting people off from my life - because my gut instinct tells me that I'm not starting from scratch and alone in this rebuilding journey this time.

## Vulnerability as a strength - or a weapon against you?

The idea that vulnerability is a sign of weakness is deeply ingrained in many professional cultures. I've carried this belief for years, convinced that showing any form of uncertainty, emotional response or human frailty would undermine my authority and competence in professional settings. I've lived with this fear for so long that I didn't even realize how much it was holding me back until I started to question my split personas that I'm maintaining in my personal and professional spheres.

It's true that vulnerability forms the very foundation of authentic leadership and meaningful connections. However, the paradox is that vulnerability can be both a strength and a weapon, depending on how it's framed and who receives it. In a culture that rewards certainty and control, showing vulnerability can feel like exposing a wound. Yet, when shared thoughtfully, vulnerability humanizes us and invites collaboration rather than competition.

One of the most powerful lessons I've learned is that vulnerability requires context. It's not about broadcasting every insecurity or personal crisis, but about sharing the moments that shaped my decisions and growth.

In the end, vulnerability is not a liability to be hidden. It's a bridge that connects my personal experiences to the universal challenges we all face in our careers. By embracing it, I find that I'm not only more authentic but also more resilient—ready to navigate the next turbulence with greater self-awareness and empathy.
